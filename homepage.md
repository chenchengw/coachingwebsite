## HERO SECTION

### Headline
**"You need to be more assertive to get to the next level."**

### Body
I still remember how that feedback landed.

It wasn't negative. But it felt like a puzzle I didn't have the instruction manual for.

I had built my leadership around collaboration, openness, and trust. People felt comfortable bringing ideas and challenges to me. I believed in collective intelligence and thoughtful decision-making.

And suddenly, it felt like those strengths were being interpreted as hesitation.

I didn't want to become someone who dominated conversations.
I didn't want to sound aggressive or dismissive.
But I also didn't want to be overlooked.

If any of that sounds familiar — I understand. Because I've been there. Many times.

I spent a decade leading engineering teams at LinkedIn and Instacart. 50+ engineers. Reorgs, roadmap battles, executive politics.

I was often the only woman at the table. I learned how to take up space — not by becoming louder, but by becoming clearer about who I already was.

Along the way, I mentored dozens of women in tech and ran women-in-tech communities. Across hundreds of those conversations, I kept hearing the same patterns. The same doubts. The same double binds. Brilliant women shrinking instead of leading — not because they lacked capability, but because no one had helped them see what was already there.

And right now, with AI accelerating everything — more reorgs, more role changes, more uncertainty — these patterns are showing up faster and hitting harder than ever.

That's what made me realize coaching was where I could have the most impact.

## "WHAT I HELP YOU SEE" SECTION

### Headline
**Every transition rewrites the rules. Nobody hands you the new ones.**

### Body
I was promoted five times in ten years. Each time, the job changed completely — and the things that got me there were almost irrelevant to what came next. Each time, I had to rewrite who I was as a leader. Now I'm doing it again, going from corporate to building something new.

Here's what I've learned: the hardest part of any transition isn't the new skills. It's the identity shift. And most people try to push through it alone — because at your level, you're not supposed to still be figuring it out.

I had great mentors who gave me advice. I had sponsors who opened doors. But through every transition — especially as the loneliness got heavier — I wished I'd had a coach. Someone who wasn't my boss, wasn't my mentor, wasn't invested in my org. Just someone who'd navigated these exact identity rewrites and could sit with me in the middle of one.

That person didn't exist for me. I want to be that person for you.

**What I help you see:**

- The transition you're actually in — not the one on your job description, but the one that's quietly rewriting what's expected of you
- The invisible rules of your new level — the ones nobody explains because everyone assumes you already know
- The stories you're telling yourself that used to help but are now holding you back
- The career taxes you're paying without realizing it — over-preparing, over-functioning, avoiding the conversations that matter most
- The difference between imposter syndrome and a real skill gap — because they feel identical from the inside

## ABOUT SECTION

### Headline
**I've rewritten my identity five times. I'm doing it again right now.**

### Body
I spent a decade in engineering leadership at LinkedIn and Instacart — from building a B2B product into a billion-dollar business to leading organizations of 50+ engineers through reorgs, roadmap battles, and executive politics.

I was promoted five times in ten years. Each time, I had to become a different kind of leader. The skills that earned the promotion were almost irrelevant to the job I got promoted into.

IC to Tech Lead — the moment it stopped being about my code.
Tech Lead to Manager — the scoreboard changed and nobody told me.
Manager to Senior Manager — my job became about other people's success, and that was a grief I didn't expect.
Senior Manager to Director — the politics weren't optional anymore. They were the job.
Director to Executive — I felt the loneliness firsthand, and I could see it was even heavier for the senior executives above me. I had mentors, sponsors, and I was still performing well — but inside, the emotional cost was compounding. I could see the full path ahead, and I chose differently while I still could.

Each of those transitions forced me to let go of something I was good at and become someone I didn't know how to be yet. I navigated all of them as the only woman and minority at the table — which meant the margin for figuring it out in public felt thinner.

Now I'm rewriting my identity again — leaving corporate to build something new. That decision, and the disorientation that comes with it, keeps me honest as a coach. I'm not someone who "figured it out" and now dispenses wisdom. I'm someone who keeps walking into the unknown and has learned how to find her footing.

Along the way, I've mentored dozens of women in tech and built communities where leaders could be honest about what they were going through. Across hundreds of those conversations, I kept seeing the same patterns — the same transition moments, the same invisible rules, the same costly lessons that didn't have to take years. And the same loneliness — even among people with great mentors and sponsors.

I coach from pattern recognition — not frameworks or clichés. When you describe your situation, I've usually lived a version of it. And I want to be the person I wish I'd had — someone who can sit with you in the middle of the transition and help you see what took me years to figure out alone.

## KEY DIFFERENTIATORS (updated for v4)

1. **Lessons, not pain points**: The site is organized around Amy's hard-won insights — not a list of problems. This positions her as someone with earned wisdom, not just empathy for struggles.

2. **Identity rewriting as the through-line**: Every transition is an identity rewrite. This is a fresh frame that goes deeper than "leadership coaching" — it names the actual experience of growth that most people suffer through alone.

3. **Still in it**: Amy isn't coaching from a finished story. She's rewriting her identity right now (corporate → startup). This makes her relatable and honest in a way that "I've figured it out" coaches can't match.

4. **The five-level map**: IC → TL → Manager → Senior Manager → Director → Exec. Any reader can find themselves on this ladder, see what's coming next, and understand why Amy is credible at every rung.

5. **Inner game + operational credibility**: NLP training gives Amy depth on the identity/pattern level. A decade at LinkedIn and Instacart gives her credibility on the tactical level. Most coaches have one or the other.

6. **Women in tech as lens, not topic**: The site speaks naturally to women in tech through Amy's story and examples — but the lessons are about leadership transitions, not gender. This attracts the primary audience without limiting the brand.

7. **Direct, no-cliché tone**: No "lean in," no "feminine energy," no "unlock your potential." Amy's voice is: here's what I learned, here's what it cost, here's what I'd tell you.
